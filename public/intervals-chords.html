01 — Foundations

What an interval actually is

An interval is the distance between two pitches, measured in semitones (half-steps). That's the whole idea — everything else is naming convention. A piano has twelve semitones before a pitch repeats an octave higher, so there are thirteen interval sizes worth naming, from standing still (0 semitones) to a full octave (12 semitones).

A semitone is the smallest distance in standard Western tuning — one key to the very next key on a piano, black or white. A whole tone is two semitones. Major scales are built from a specific pattern of whole tones and semitones, which is exactly why they all sound like "major" no matter which note they start on — the pattern of distances is identical, only the starting pitch moves.

Past the octave, the same names repeat with "compound" versions: a 9th is an octave + a 2nd, an 11th is an octave + a 4th, a 13th is an octave + a 6th. Jazz chord extensions (9, 11, 13) are just these compound intervals stacked on top of a seventh chord — the same intervals from this table, one octave up.

03 — Where intervals live

Scales are just a fixed sequence of intervals

Change the root and the notes change, but if the formula is the same, every interval relationship inside it — tonic to 3rd, tonic to 5th, 3rd to 5th — stays exactly the same. 04 — Stacking

A chord is intervals stacked, not notes chosen

Take a root, add a note a third above it, add another note a third above that, and you have a triad. The only thing that changes between a major, minor, diminished, or augmented triad is which size of third gets stacked where. Do it once more and you have a seventh chord.

06 — Chords living inside a scale

The chords a scale gives you for free

Pick any note in a scale as a root, and stack the notes a third and a fifth above it — using only notes already in that scale. Do this on every degree and you get that scale's diatonic chords: the built-in harmony of the key. Choose a root and a scale to generate its full set, triads and sevenths, for every degree.

This is why a major key's harmony leans so hard on I, IV, and V (all major), why ii, iii, and vi feel like the minor detours, and why vii° is the one chord in the key that's genuinely unstable and wants to resolve. Switch the scale to harmonic minor and watch V turn major (thanks to the raised 7th) — that single change is why harmonic minor exists: it gives minor keys a proper dominant.
07 — Tying it together

Intervals are the DNA. Chords are the organism.

Every chord quality, decoded by ear

Once you can hear the difference between a major 3rd and a minor 3rd, you can identify almost any triad by ear — the fifth barely varies, but the third decides everything.

Inversions: an interval flipped upside down

Move the lower note of an interval up an octave (or the upper note down one) and you get its inversion. Every interval and its inversion add up to 12 semitones. This is also why a major 3rd below a note sounds like a minor 6th above it — same two pitches, same relationship, viewed from the other end.

09 — Navigation

Circle of Fifths & Circle of Fourths

Both circles arrange the twelve major keys around a clock face. The circle of fifths moves clockwise by a perfect fifth (7 semitones) at each step; the circle of fourths moves clockwise by a perfect fourth (5 semitones) — the same circle, read in reverse. Relative minor keys appear in the inner ring; key signatures in the centre.

Circle of Fifths

Each step clockwise raises the key by a perfect fifth (+7 semitones) and adds one sharp — or removes one flat.

Circle of Fourths

Each step clockwise raises the key by a perfect fourth (+5 semitones) and adds one flat — or removes one sharp.

Key signature mnemonic — sharps: Father Charles Goes Down And Ends Battle (F C G D A E B). Each word gives the next sharp added as you move clockwise. Flats: Battle Ends And Down Goes Charles Father — the same sentence reversed (B E A D G C F). Neighbouring keys on either circle share all but one note, which is why moving between them sounds smooth.
